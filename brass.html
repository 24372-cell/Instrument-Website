Strings

Explore the instruments in the string family

Trumpet

The trumpet is the bold, gleaming leader of the brass family, instantly recognizable by its coiled brass tubing, flared bell, and three shiny button valves.Unlike woodwinds, it does not use a reed; instead, the player creates a sound by buzzing their lips against a cup-shaped metal mouthpiece. As the highest-pitched instrument in the brass section, it produces a bright, piercing, and triumphant tone that easily cuts through an entire orchestra. It is incredibly versatile, serving as the heroic voice of classical fanfares, the driving force of driving jazz solos, and a punchy staple in modern pop and funk brass sections.

Trombone

The trombone is a powerful and distinctive member of the brass family, instantly recognizable by its long, U-shaped outer slide instead of the button valves found on most other brass instruments.Like the trumpet, it doesn't use a reed; the musician creates a sound by buzzing their lips into a large metal mouthpiece. By smoothly moving the slide in and out, the player changes the length of the tubing to alter the pitch, allowing the trombone to seamlessly glide between notes in a unique effect called a glissandro. It produces a deep, rich, and commanding tone that provides the heavy, heroic backbone for symphony orchestras, marching bands, and jazz ensembles alike.

French Horn

The French horn is a beautiful, coiled member of the brass family that is famous for its large, flared bell and its incredibly wide, expressive range.Unlike the trumpet or trombone, it features a series of rotary valves pressed with the left hand, while the player's right hand sits inside the bell to adjust the tone and pitch. The musician buzzes their lips into a small, funnel-shaped mouthpiece, sending air through nearly 4 metres of tightly wrapped tubing to produce a sound that can shift from a soft, velvety whisper to a bright, heroic roar. Because its rich tone blends perfectly with both woodwinds and strings, it is a vital bridge in the symphony orchestra and is famously used in cinema to play soaring, adventurous movie themes.

Euphonium

The euphonium is a large, teardrop-shaped member of the brass family that looks like a miniature tuba and derives its name from a Greek word meaning "sweet-voiced".Like other brass instruments, the player creates a sound by buzzing their lips into a large, cup-shaped mouthpiece, pressing three or four piston valves to change notes. It features a wide, conical bore—meaning the tubing gradually stretches wider from the mouthpiece to the bell—which gives it an incredibly dark, velvety, and rich tone. While it rarely appears in standard symphony orchestras, the euphonium is the absolute superstar soloist of brass bands and marching bands, treasured for its ability to play fast, acrobatic melodies with a warm, cello-like sound.

Tuba

The tuba is the massive, heavy anchor of the brass family, serving as the largest and lowest-pitched instrument in the entire orchestra.Like the euphonium, it features a wide, conical shape and a massive upward-pointing bell, requiring the player to buzz their lips into a large, deep mouthpiece while pressing heavy piston or rotary valves. Because it requires an immense volume of air to fill its massive frame, it produces a deep, rumbling, and powerful sound that provides the rhythmic foundation for symphony orchestras, marching bands, and dixieland jazz groups. Despite its giant size and booming bass notes, a skilled tuba player can make the instrument sound surprisingly agile, warm, and lyrical.
