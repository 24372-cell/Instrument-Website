Strings

Explore the instruments in the string family

Violin

The violin is a four-stringed, bowed acoustic instrument that plays the highest notes in the string family. To understand it fully, you must know its physical construction, essential gear, and maintenance needs. A standard violin consists of a hollow wooden body—traditionally built with a spruce top for resonance and maple sides and back—along with a neck, fingerboard, bridge, and pegbox. Sound is produced when you draw a bow across the strings or pluck them, sending vibrations through the bridge into the body to amplify the tone. You cannot play the violin alone; you also need a functional bow (made of wood or carbon fiber), rosin (a sticky sap applied to the bow hair to create friction against the strings), a shoulder rest for comfortable posture, and a digital tuner or pitch pipe.

Viola

The viola is a bowed string instrument that sits directly between the violin and the cello in both size and pitch. Visually similar to the violin, the viola is slightly larger and built with thicker strings, which produces a much deeper, warmer, and more mellow tone. It is tuned a perfect fifth lower than a violin (C3, G3, D4, A4), allowing it to bridge the harmonic gap between the high-register violins and the low-register cellos within an orchestra or string quartet. While historically relegated to filling out accompaniment harmonies, the viola has earned a rich repertoire of its own, celebrated by composers for its expressive, soulful, and dark timber.

Cello

The cello (or violoncello) is a bowed string instrument that plays a vital role in orchestral, chamber, and solo music due to its massive pitch range and expressive power. It is much larger than the violin and viola, requiring the musician to sit down and prop the instrument on the floor using a metal endpin. Tuned exactly one octave lower than the viola (C2, G2, D3, A3), the cello delivers a deep, resonant, and rich bass tone while remaining fully capable of soaring into lyrical, tenor melodies. Because its sound closely matches the pitch range of the human singing voice, the cello is widely considered one of the most emotional and versatile instruments in the world.

Double Bass

The double bass (also called the contrabass or upright bass) is the largest and lowest-pitched bowed string instrument in the modern symphony orchestra. Standing over six feet tall, it requires the player to stand or sit on a high stool, supporting the heavy body with a sturdy metal endpin. Unlike the violin, viola, and cello, the double bass is traditionally tuned in perfect fourths (E1, A1, D2, G2) rather than fifths, a design that makes its massive scale fingerings easier to manage. It provides the foundational harmonic weight and rhythmic pulse for classical orchestras, but it is equally legendary in jazz, bluegrass, and rockabilly, where players often ditch the bow to pluck the strings using the "slap" or pizzicato technique.
